Collaboration AI Blogs

I spent the first year of my AI blog doing what every "growth expert" tells you to do: publish 2,500-word tutorials solo, three times a week, and pray to the Google gods. After 12 months and 147 articles, I had 1,200 monthly readers and a backlink profile that looked like a ghost town.

Then I co-wrote one article with a friend who runs a small ML newsletter. We benchmarked five open-source LLMs against GPT-4 on a coding task nobody else had tested. That single post got more backlinks in 30 days than my previous year of publishing combined — 23 links, including one from a university lab page and two from mid-tier tech magazines.

That's when I stopped writing alone.

Here's what nobody tells you about the AI niche in 2026: there are maybe 300 serious AI blogs on the internet, and they all know each other. The ones growing fastest aren't the ones with the best technical SEO — they're the ones collaborating constantly. They co-publish benchmarks, swap expert quotes, cross-promote newsletters, and turn one piece of research into six different formats across four different audiences.

This guide is what I learned from the last 18 months of doing that — the good, the bad, and the collaborations that flopped completely.

💡 Reality check: Collaborative content earns 2-3x more backlinks than solo posts — because every partner has a built-in reason to share, link, and cite it. One well-executed co-authored study can outperform an entire quarter of solo publishing.

What Content Collaboration Actually Looks Like (Not the Theory)

Forget the textbook definition. In the AI niche, content collaboration usually means one of these scenarios:

You and someone else each bring something the other doesn't have. Maybe they have an audience of 20,000 newsletter subscribers and you have original benchmark data. Maybe they have access to a private Discord of ML researchers and you have a writing voice people actually enjoy. Maybe you're both small but cover different sub-niches — you do LLMs, they do computer vision.

The point isn't to "leverage synergies." The point is that neither of you could make this piece alone, so you both get something you couldn't have gotten solo.

The Only 4 Collaboration Types Worth Your Time

I've tried about a dozen formats over the last two years. Most were wastes of time. These four actually worked:

FormatWhat It IsEffortPayoff
Co-authored researchJoint data study or benchmarkHighMassive links + citations
Expert roundupsMultiple voices on one questionMediumShares + relationships
Guest exchangesYou write for them, they write for youLowSteady contextual backlinks
Newsletter swapsRecommend each other's issuesLowSubscriber growth

1. Co-authored benchmark studies

This is the highest-effort, highest-reward play. You and a partner each contribute data, analysis, or expertise to produce something neither could publish alone. Our LLM benchmark with my friend worked because I had the engineering chops and he had the distribution.

2. Expert roundups with real names attached

Not the garbage "20 experts share their top SEO tip" roundups that plague the web. I mean roundups where you personally know every contributor, the question is genuinely interesting, and every answer adds something new. In the AI niche, questions like "Which open-source model would you deploy in production tomorrow and why?" actually get good answers because researchers have opinions.

3. Guest post swaps

You write for their audience, they write for yours. Simple. But the key word is "complementary" — don't swap with someone covering the exact same topics as you. If you write about LLMs, swap with someone who covers AI hardware or AI regulation.

4. Newsletter cross-promotions

Honestly the lowest-effort, highest-ROI play I've found. You mention their newsletter in yours, they mention yours in theirs. I've done maybe 40 of these in the last year, and the average one brings me 80-150 new subscribers in a week.

The Formats I Tried and Regret

For balance, here's what didn't work for me:

  • Co-hosted webinars: Took 15 hours to prepare, got 34 live attendees. Never again.
  • Joint Twitter/X Spaces: Audience overlap was too high; most listeners were already following both of us.
  • Generic "expert roundup" posts with 40 contributors: I got three good quotes, 37 forgettable ones, and spent weeks chasing people. The finished piece read like a directory.
  • Podcast guest swaps: Only works if both podcasts already have strong distribution. If you're both under 1,000 listeners, you're just shouting into each other's voids.

Finding Partners Who Actually Want to Collaborate

This is where most people waste months. They DM 200 people with "let's collab!" and get 200 polite declines. Here's what actually works in the AI niche:

  • Who's already linking to you? Check Ahrefs or Semrush. If someone linked to your post unprompted, they already like your work. They're your easiest collaboration target.
  • The guest post sites you've pitched to: Our list of AI niche guest post sites is full of editors who accept external contributors. A successful guest post is a warm introduction for a future collab.
  • Newsletters you already read: If you're subscribed to someone's AI newsletter and genuinely enjoy it, you're their ideal collaborator. Reach out as a reader first, not a marketer.
  • GitHub contributors: People maintaining popular ML repos are often happy to co-author technical content — they have deep expertise but usually hate writing.

🤝 The "three-touch" rule: I never pitch a collaboration to someone I haven't interacted with at least three times before. Comment on their posts. Share their work. Reply thoughtfully to their newsletter. Then, and only then, send the collab pitch. This sounds slow, but it converts at maybe 40% vs. 3% for cold outreach.

What a Fair Collaboration Deal Looks Like

Most collaborations fail because the deal is vague. Here's what I actually negotiate, in writing, before we start:

TopicWhat to Agree On
Credit orderWho's listed first as author (alternate on future pieces)
Link splitsEach party gets 2-3 contextual links to their own content, natural anchors only
PromotionBoth share on social within 24h; both send in next newsletter
RepublishingCan either party republish on Medium/Substack after 60 days?
Follow-upBoth commit to linking to the piece from at least one future solo article

I learned the hard way on the last point: I once co-wrote a great piece, my partner never linked to it from anything else, and the piece died in three weeks. Now I put "two follow-up links within 90 days" in writing.

The 30-Day Sequence That Actually Gets Replies

This is the exact sequence I use when approaching someone new. It's slow, and that's the point.

  • Days 1-5: Subscribe to their newsletter, follow them on Twitter/X, leave two thoughtful comments on their recent posts. Don't ask for anything.
  • Days 6-10: Share one of their pieces with your audience, tag them, add a genuine take. They'll usually thank you or engage.
  • Days 11-14: Send a short email (under 100 words) mentioning a specific piece of theirs you liked and why. No ask.
  • Days 15-21: Reply to something they post with a useful addition — a counter-example, a related paper, a tool they might not know.
  • Days 22-30: Now, and only now, pitch the collab. Keep it specific: "I've been thinking about [specific idea], and I think your expertise + my angle could make something neither of us could do alone."

Mistakes I've Made (So You Don't Have To)

  • Collaborating with someone who doesn't reply to emails promptly: If they're slow before the project starts, they'll ghost you during it. Walk away.
  • Not defining "done": One partner thought the piece was done at 1,500 words, the other expected 4,000. We argued for a week and the piece never published.
  • Co-authoring with a competitor: I did this once. We both ended up competing for the same keywords with near-identical content. Lost rankings on both sides.
  • Over-promising in the pitch: "This will go viral!" No, it won't. Promise a good piece of content, not fame.

Fitting Collaboration Into Your Larger Strategy

Collaboration is a growth lever, not a standalone strategy. It only works if you have a foundation:

  • Solid solo content: You need a portfolio that proves you're worth collaborating with. Our guide on the best AI content strategy for SEO shows how to build that foundation first.
  • An editorial system: Collaborations take extra coordination time, so your solo publishing needs to run smoothly in the background. The AI blog SEO strategy for 2026 maps that system end-to-end.
  • Relationships with editors: Guest posting on tech websites is usually how you build those first relationships, which later turn into collaborations.

Do these three things first. Add collaboration on top. That's how the fastest-growing AI blogs actually grow — they don't replace solo publishing with collaborations, they stack them.

The Honest Truth About Collaboration

Look, collaboration isn't magic. Most of your collabs will be modest wins — a few hundred extra readers, a couple of backlinks, a new professional relationship. One in ten will be a home run that changes your trajectory. The ones that flop will teach you more than the ones that succeed.

But here's what I know for sure after 18 months of doing this: the AI bloggers who grow the fastest are the ones who build in public with other people. Solo publishing has a ceiling. Collaboration doesn't. Start small. One newsletter swap. One guest post exchange. Learn what works for your niche and your voice. Then scale the formats that do.
