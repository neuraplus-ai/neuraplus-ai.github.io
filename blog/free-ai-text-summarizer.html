A summarizer is the fastest way to find out whether a ten-page reading is worth your evening. It's also the easiest way to walk into class confidently wrong, because a summary that sounds smooth can still leave out the one sentence the whole argument depends on. The free options differ in a way most reviews gloss over: some pick sentences from your text, others rewrite it, and that difference decides how much you can trust the result. Here's how the free tools compare, what their limits really are, and how to check a summary in under two minutes.

What's the best free AI text summarizer?

For pasted text, QuillBot's free summarizer handles up to 1,200 words with two output modes. For long PDFs and your own study material, Google's NotebookLM is the usual free pick. A general chatbot such as Claude or ChatGPT is the most flexible, since you can say exactly what the summary is for. Always check it against the original.

Two kinds of summarizer, and why it matters

The demo above is extractive: it scores each sentence by how many of the text's common words it contains and keeps the top few. Everything in the result is a sentence the author actually wrote, so it can't invent anything. The downside is clunky flow and missing context.

Most modern AI tools are abstractive. They read the text and write new sentences about it, which gives smoother summaries and also opens the door to errors, because the model can state something the source never said. Neither is better in every case. Extractive is safer for facts, and abstractive is nicer to read.

The two-minute check for any summary

  1. Find the thesis. Does the summary state the author's main claim? If not, it missed the point.
  2. Spot-check three facts. Pick a number, a name and a claim from the summary and find each in the original.
  3. Look for what's missing. Read the original's first and last paragraphs. Anything important there that the summary skipped?
  4. Check the hedges. "May," "suggests" and "in some cases" often disappear, turning a cautious finding into a bold one.
  5. Never cite the summary. Cite the original, after you've read the part you're using.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Using a summary instead of reading the section you plan to quote. Summaries compress, and compression drops caveats. Read the real paragraph before it goes in your paper.

Prompts that make chatbot summaries better

A plain "summarize this" gets you a generic result. Giving the summary a purpose changes it a lot.

GoalAdd this to your prompt
Quick understandingSummarize in five bullet points for someone who hasn't read it.
Study notesList the key terms with a one-line definition each, using only the text.
Decision makingWhat are the three strongest arguments, and the two weakest?
Fact-checkingQuote the sentence from the text that supports each point.
Exam prepTurn this into ten questions I could be asked, with answers from the text.

The "quote the sentence" trick is the most useful one. It forces the tool to point at real text, and any claim it can't quote is a claim to double-check. For more study-ready prompts in this style, see the Claude prompts for students guide.

Build your own summary checker

I will paste an original text and then a summary of it. Act as a careful
fact-checker. Do not write a new summary.

For the summary, tell me:
1. Whether it states the author's main claim.
2. Any claim in the summary that is NOT supported by the original,
   quoting the closest sentence from the original.
3. Any important point in the original that the summary left out.
4. Any place where a cautious claim became a certain one.
