Google Widgets Dice

Type "roll a dice" into Google and a die appears at the top of the results, ready to roll. No app, no download, no loose dice rolling under the couch. It's one of the most useful little widgets Google runs, and most people only discover it when they've lost the dice from a board game box.

How to use Google roll a dice

Search for "roll a dice" or "roll a die" on Google. A die widget appears above the normal results. Click or tap the die to roll it, and the number lands after a short animation. Click again to roll again. If your region shows a dropdown, you can switch to a different die type, such as a 20-sided one.

That's the entire feature. There's nothing to install and nothing to sign in to, and it works on phones, tablets and computers. You can also try it with your voice by asking your phone's assistant to roll a die.

Step by step

  1. Open Google in any browser, or the Google app.
  2. Type roll a dice or roll a die and search.
  3. Look for the die at the top of the page.
  4. Click or tap it to roll.
  5. If a dropdown is shown, choose the die type you need, then roll.

⚠️ What goes wrong: Nothing appears. That usually means the search was phrased oddly or your results are showing a different layout. Search the exact phrase again, check that you're on Google and not another search engine, and try a different browser if the widget still doesn't show.

Which dice can you roll?

The standard six-sided die is the default. Depending on where you are and what Google is showing, you may be able to pick other types for tabletop games. If you don't see the dropdown, use the six-sided die and convert the result, or roll twice for larger ranges.

Die Faces Common Use
d4 4 Small damage rolls in tabletop role-playing games
d6 6 Board games like Monopoly and Ludo, plus most family games
d8 8 Medium damage rolls
d10 10 Percentile rolls, usually in pairs
d12 12 Heavy weapon damage
d20 20 Attack and skill checks in Dungeons & Dragons

What are the odds?

A fair six-sided die gives every face a 1 in 6 chance, about 16.7 percent. The more interesting math starts when you roll two.

Two Dice Total Ways to Roll It Chance
2 or 12 1 2.8%
3 or 11 2 5.6%
4 or 10 3 8.3%
5 or 9 4 11.1%
6 or 8 5 13.9%
7 6 16.7%

🎯 Why seven rules Monopoly: That's why seven is the most common total in games like Monopoly, and why snake eyes (two ones) is rare. A 20-sided die gives you a flat 5 percent chance for any single number, so rolling a natural 20 or a natural 1 each happens once in twenty rolls on average.

Streaks look suspicious but are normal. The chance of rolling the same number twice in a row on a six-sided die is 1 in 6. Three times in a row is 1 in 36. If your group claims the online die is rigged because someone rolled three sixes, remember that 1 in 36 happens to somebody eventually.

Is Google's die fair?

Google's widget uses a random-number process, and for games, classroom use and everyday decisions, it behaves like a fair die. You don't need to worry about weighted faces or wobbly edges. What you can't do is verify the source code, so don't use it for anything with money, legal weight or a formal lottery. For those, use a certified method.

One quirk of real life is worth knowing. Physical dice aren't always perfect, and cheap ones can favor certain faces if they're poorly made. A digital die has no such manufacturing flaw, which makes it a decent tiebreaker when you don't trust the old plastic die from the bottom of the closet.

When Google's die beats real dice, and when it doesn't

🎲 My view: Keep Google's die on your phone for emergencies and quick decisions, and keep a real set for game night. Watching a die tumble across the table is half the fun of Dungeons & Dragons.

Fun ways to use it

🎓 Classroom probability lesson

Have students predict totals for ten rolls and compare them with the chart above. The gap between their predictions and the math sticks with them.

🎲 Board game tiebreaker

Settle who goes first in a board game without arguing. One high roll, and the argument ends.

🍽️ Restaurant roulette

Assign each number an option for chores, picking a movie, or choosing which restaurant to try. No more "I don't mind" for twenty minutes.

🪙 Combine with other tools

If a die feels like too many choices, the Google flip a coin tool gives you a clean yes or no. And if you're sharing a screen with friends and want something more active than rolling, try beating each other's scores in the Chrome dino game.

Other quick tools hiding in Google Search

Rolling a die is one of several small widgets Google builds into search results, alongside timers, calculators and unit converters. If you like finding these, read through the Google easter eggs list for 2026 and the guide to cool things to type in Google.

✅ Bottom line: Type roll a dice into Google and you have an instant, fair virtual die with no installs and no sign-in. It's perfect for lost board game pieces, classroom probability demos, and quick yes-or-no decisions — but keep real dice around for game night, because the tumble is half the fun.
