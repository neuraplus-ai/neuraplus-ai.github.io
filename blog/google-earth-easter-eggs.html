Google Earth Flight Simulator Easter Eggs

For almost twenty years, the best thing hidden in Google Earth was a flight simulator that you could only find if someone told you the secret keys. In June 2026 Google stopped hiding it. The simulator now runs in a web browser, and anyone can open it from a menu. That makes it the headline of this guide, but the story behind it, and a few smaller Earth surprises, are worth knowing too.

How do you use the Google Earth flight simulator?

Open Google Earth in a web browser at earth.google.com, click Explore Earth, search for the place where you want to start, open the Tools menu, and choose Flight simulator at the bottom. Your plane appears in the air. Steer with the arrow keys or mouse, and change speed with Page Up and Page Down.

🚀 Quick Start

Go to earth.google.com, click Explore Earth, search for a city, then choose Tools → Flight simulator. No download, no subscription.

Google announced the web version on June 12, 2026, saying it's designed for casual exploration rather than serious flight training. It also labeled the feature experimental, so expect rough edges and possible changes.

Controls vary by device and version, and reports disagree on the exact keys: one source lists arrow keys or mouse to turn and Page Up and Page Down for throttle, while another describes Up and Down for thrust. Trust the on-screen prompt.

⚠️ Common Mistake: Over-steering. The controls are sensitive, and a big mouse movement sends the plane into a roll or a spin. Make small corrections and let the plane settle.

The original secret: Ctrl+Alt+A

The flight simulator first appeared in Google Earth version 4.2 in 2007 as a genuine hidden feature. To open it on the desktop application, you pressed Ctrl+Alt+A on Windows or Command+Option+A on a Mac. A menu then let you pick one of two aircraft, an F-16 and a four-seat SR22, and start from the user's location or from a list of airports.

Google's own Maps blog confirmed it in September 2007, describing why it had been hidden and adding a second way in. If the menu item wasn't there, you could type the word "Lilienthal" into the Fly To box, which did the same thing as the key combination.

History timeline

The hidden feature ships in version 4.2

Google Earth 4.2 includes a flight simulator accessible only via Ctrl+Alt+A (Windows) or Command+Option+A (Mac). The secret word "Lilienthal" works in the Fly To box as an alternative entry.

2007

In September 2007, Google's own Maps blog writes about the simulator and explains why it was hidden, turning a secret into an official announcement.

2026

On June 12, 2026, Google launches the flight simulator in the browser version of Google Earth, available to all users without a download. The feature is labeled "experimental."

Other things hiding in Google Earth and Street View

Smaller surprises have come and gone around the Earth family of products. Pegman, the orange figure you drag onto a map to open Street View, has dressed up for holidays: a heart on Valentine's Day, a snowman before Christmas and a witch on a broomstick for Halloween, according to a PC-Welt report. Those are occasional, so don't count on seeing them on any given day. The Google Maps easter eggs guide covers the Pegman costume changes and the Area 51 UFO in more detail.

Google Earth has also added playful features outside of hidden menus, such as historical views of select places in 2024, according to Engadget. Those aren't easter eggs, but they reward the same kind of curiosity.

What the flight simulator can't do

Set your expectations. It's a casual simulator. It doesn't model realistic aerodynamics, there are no missions or scores, and bad bandwidth can make buildings pop in slowly. Treat it as a way to see the planet from above, not as a replacement for a dedicated flight game.
