Family Google Tricks

Kids love Google tricks for the same reason adults do: you type a few words and the screen does something it shouldn't. The difference is that a child clicks on whatever shows up first, and some of the pages that claim to be "Google tricks" are third-party sites packed with ads. So the useful question isn't only "what's fun?" but "what's fun and safe to hand over?" This guide sorts twelve tricks into those that run on Google itself and those that need a closer look.

What are the best Google tricks for kids?

The best Google tricks for kids are the ones that run inside Google Search or Chrome with nothing to install: the built-in Snake, Pac-Man, tic tac toe and solitaire games, a virtual die and coin, the barrel roll effect, and the Chrome dinosaur game. They're free, quick to start, and don't send a child to unfamiliar websites.

🚀 Quick Start: Open Google, type snake game or tic tac toe, and tap Play. Sit with younger children for the first session and set a timer before they begin.

The first three to try with a young child

Start with tic tac toe. It's a two-player game with rules a five-year-old already knows, and Google lets you pick an easy level. Then try the die and coin widgets for family board games when the real dice have vanished, which they always do. For a short burst of silliness, show them the barrel roll trick, where the whole results page spins once and stops.

Tricks that turn into lessons

A few of these quietly teach something.

The die is a gentle probability lesson. Ask your child to guess how often a six appears in ten rolls, then roll and count. The coin shows that a streak of heads doesn't make tails "due." Google Earth's flight simulator, which Google brought to the web in June 2026, is a geography lesson in disguise: ask them to fly to your hometown, then to somewhere with mountains, then to somewhere on the coast. It's described as a casual simulator, and the controls take practice, so expect crashes and laughter.

A parent's safety checklist

You don't need to be technical for this. Run through it once and you're set.

  • Check the address bar. If it doesn't say google.com or another Google domain, it's a third-party page. Look at it before your child does.
  • Turn on SafeSearch. Google's SafeSearch filters explicit results from search. Find the switch in Google's search settings or in Google's SafeSearch help pages.
  • Skip sign-in on any mirror site. Nothing in a trick needs a login.
  • Avoid downloads and pop-ups. Close any page that offers an "update" or a "player."
  • Use a child account if your kid has their own device. Google offers family tools such as Family Link for supervised accounts.
  • Keep the device in a shared room. Younger children do better with a screen where you can glance at it.

None of this makes the trick pages dangerous. It just keeps the fun separate from the ad-heavy corners of the web.

💡 How to avoid it: For the mirror-site tricks, open them yourself first, close any ads, and hand over the tab only when it's clean. Better still, try them on your own device and let your child watch.

Build your own: a printable scavenger hunt

This guide's twist is a paper activity. Paste the prompt below into Claude or any AI chat to get a printable "Google tricks passport" that sends your child hunting for tricks and stamping each one off.

You are a children's activity designer. Create a one-page printable
"Google Tricks Passport" for a [age]-year-old.

Include:
- A title and a place to write the child's name
- 8 boxes, each with a short instruction such as "Search 'roll a dice'
  and roll a 6" or "Play one round of tic tac toe"
- A tick box and a stamp circle for every challenge
- One bonus box called "Teach a grown-up a trick"

Rules:
- Use only tricks that run on Google Search or in Chrome.
- Write at a [reading level] reading level, max 12 words per instruction.
- Add one line at the bottom: "Ask a grown-up before clicking anything new."
- Format it as a clean table I can paste into a document and print.

Swap the age and reading level, and you get a different sheet every time.

Keep going with the next set

When your child outgrows the basics, the Google Gravity trick is the natural next stop, though it lives on a mirror site, so use the checklist above. For a calmer option, the Google Underwater trick turns the page into a fish tank. Two longer games that reward practice are the Google Pac-Man game and the Google Snake game tricks.

How this guide was checked

I compared Google's own pages and several news sources, confirmed which tricks run on Google itself, and flagged the ones that rely on mirror sites. Google can change or retire a trick without notice, so a game that doesn't load isn't a sign you did something wrong. Try it yourself before you hand the device over.
